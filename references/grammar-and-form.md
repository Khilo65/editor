# Grammar and form review

Use these checks when reviewing sentence grammar, quotations, citations, or document form. Diagnose the actual construction before changing it. Preserve meaning, quoted text, exact names and titles, adopted terminology, and explicitly protected wording. Make the smallest correction that solves the defect. The selected house restrictions govern word choice, phrase bans, qualification necessity, punctuation, and voice; a conventional alternative does not override an explicit restriction. Apply [Q1](model-style-review.md#q1-keep-only-necessary-qualifications) to optional qualifications without deleting necessary grammatical scope or factual limits.

## Noun forms and list punctuation

- Form the singular possessive with apostrophe-s, including most names ending in s. Retain the selected exceptions for ancient names ending in -es or -is, Jesus, and traditional sake expressions. An of-construction can avoid an awkward possessive. Personal possessives such as hers, its, theirs, and yours take no apostrophe. Verify the actual name before changing it.
- Use a serial comma in a series of three or more terms joined by one conjunction. Preserve the exact punctuation of an organization's official name.
- Put a comma before etc. Under the selected literal style, enclose etc. and jr. with commas unless they end the sentence. Check whether etc. is appropriate at all using the usage lookup. Do not normalize a fixed name, title, or quotation silently.

## Clause boundaries and interruptions

- Put a comma before a coordinating conjunction that joins independent clauses, including and, but, for, or, and nor. Determine clause independence from the grammar, not the presence of the conjunction alone.
- Where the second clause begins with dependent material, attach that material correctly. Do not insert a second comma merely because a dependent clause follows the conjunction. Use a connector that expresses the supported causal, concessive, or temporal relation; never invent a relation to make the transition smoother. The house restriction on while applies even to a grammatical temporal use.
- Repair a comma splice with a period, semicolon, or appropriate coordinating conjunction. A transitional adverb does not license a comma splice. Very short parallel clauses can use a deliberate comma for effect; review the effect in context instead of treating every such comma as accidental. Avoid habitual connective so.
- Use both enclosing commas around a parenthetical interruption, or neither where a light interruption permits omission. Separate supplementary relative, time, and place clauses with commas. Keep restrictive clauses attached because they select which person or thing the sentence means. Check appositives and introductory or trailing phrases in context.
- When a conjunction immediately precedes an interruption in a construction requiring the first enclosing comma before the conjunction, put it there; do not detach the conjunction mechanically from its clause.
- Repair an accidental fragment that detaches a dependent phrase or appositive from its sentence. A purposeful fragment can carry justified emphasis. Preserve an explicitly chosen fragment, judge it against the genre and neighboring sentences, and do not expand it only to regularize sentence lengths.
- Do not introduce em dashes or clause-joining colons in running prose or table cells. Preserve syntax-required punctuation, exact quotations, titles, and other protected exceptions.

### Clause and comma examples

- **Change a comma splice:** “The records are incomplete, therefore we need another search.” → “The records are incomplete; therefore, we need another search.” Change the boundary punctuation; keep the stated relation.
- **Keep a shared subject together:** “The team reviewed the file, and approved it.” → “The team reviewed the file and approved it.” The second verb does not introduce an independent clause. Remove only the unnecessary comma.
- **Resolve meaning before changing restrictive commas:** “Drivers who hold permits may park here” selects permit holders. “The drivers, who hold permits, may park here” adds that the named group all hold permits. Both can be correct. Adding or deleting commas changes the assertion, so confirm the intended group first.
- **Keep justified emphasis:** “We called twice. No answer.” can remain when the author deliberately uses the fragment for emphasis. Do not merge every fragment automatically.

## Modifier placement and clear reference

- Make the grammatical subject perform or bear the action or quality in an introductory participial phrase, adjective phrase, or related construction. If it does not, move the modifier beside its actual subject or name the supported actor in a clause. Never infer an unknown actor.
- Keep related words close. Remove needless interruptions between subject and main verb, retaining clear apposition, relative clauses, and deliberate periodic structure when they help the reader.
- Place a relative clause next to its intended antecedent. A clear appositive may intervene when it names the same person or thing and creates no ambiguity; do not move it merely to enforce adjacency. Resolve competing nouns explicitly when proximity alone leaves doubt.
- Put only, negatives, and other modifiers beside the words they govern. Preserve the scope of a denial or exception; “not all” must not become “none.” Change the smallest span that removes the ambiguity.

### Modifier examples

- **Repair a dangling modifier:** “After reading the letter, the news upset Omar.” → “After Omar read the letter, the news upset him.” Use this repair only when the context confirms Omar as the reader. If the actor is unknown, ask before naming one.
- **Preserve scope:** “Only Mira approved the draft” limits who approved it. “Mira approved only the draft” limits what Mira approved. Neither is an automatic replacement for the other. The editor needs the intended meaning before moving **only**.
- **Preserve partial negation:** “Not all members attended” allows some attendance. “No members attended” rules out any attendance. Do not strengthen the negative to shorten the wording.

## Parallel grammar

- Express coordinated ideas in equivalent grammatical form. A shared article or preposition must apply clearly to every item. Repeat it for each item, or use it once where the grammar permits.
- Align the constructions after correlative pairs. If a house restriction excludes a particular contrast formula, recast it without losing the relation.
- Keep useful local parallelism when repairing repetitive writing. Matching grammar in a list is different from telling every example in the same rhetorical template. Group a long set of equivalent ideas where helpful; use a table only when the reader and genre benefit.

### Parallelism examples

- **Delete one word:** “Staff must file receipts, to label folders, and record dates.” → “Staff must file receipts, label folders, and record dates.” Delete only **to**; retain the three actions and their order.
- **Share or repeat the preposition consistently:** “We meet in spring, summer, and in winter.” → “We meet in spring, summer, and winter.” “We meet in spring, in summer, and in winter” is also valid. Choose the smallest repair suited to the sentence's emphasis.
- **Keep an effective parallel list:** “The clerk checks names, stamps forms, and files copies.” needs no variation merely because its verbs share a grammatical pattern.

## Tense and attribution

- Keep a summary's tense coherent. Use present tense for a drama's action. Prefer present for a poem, story, or novel, or maintain a consistently chosen past. Use perfect or past perfect for the appropriate earlier action and preserve required tense in reported discourse.
- Preserve necessary historical tense and accurate time relations. Do not force one tense across passages with different purposes.
- Make clear whose ideas a summary presents. Remove redundant reporting formulas only where attribution remains clear. In analytical writing, use the evidence to develop the point instead of retelling every source sequentially. Never cut citations or source boundaries to reduce repetition.

## Parentheses and quotations

- Punctuate the surrounding sentence as though parenthetical material were absent. Omit a terminal period inside parentheses for an embedded phrase or sentence. Retain a necessary question mark or exclamation mark. A wholly independent parenthetical sentence keeps its final stop inside the closing parenthesis.
- Introduce a formal documentary quotation with a grammatical lead-in or separate sentence that meets the house punctuation restriction. Do not add a prohibited introductory colon or change punctuation inside the source quotation to avoid it.
- Use a comma before an appositive or direct-object quotation when the sentence's grammar calls for one. Do not insert a comma mechanically between a verb and an integrated quoted phrase.
- For complete verse lines, preserve lineation and use a fresh, centered line without quotation marks under the selected style. A requested publication format can specify another block treatment.
- For a quotation introduced by that, recast the lead-in where needed to keep the selected indirect-discourse convention without falsely converting a verbatim quotation into the author's own words. Do not remove source-identifying quotation marks just to regularize the construction.
- Familiar sayings, literary phrases, and colloquialisms do not need quotation marks merely because they are familiar expressions. Retain marks that signal a true quotation, a contested term, or a distinction essential to meaning.

## References and titles

- Follow the requested citation system. If a compact abbreviated-title system is in use, define repeated title abbreviations in an alphabetical list. Do not introduce that system into a document that uses another one.
- Prefer parenthetical references or footnotes to cumbersome in-sentence bibliographic detail. Keep substantive attribution in the prose where the reader needs it.
- In a compact literary reference scheme, use conventional numerals and omit repeated act, scene, or page labels except where only one locator category is given. Preserve explicit citation conventions and verified locators.
- Set literary titles in italics with appropriate initial capitals unless the target publication specifies otherwise. Interpret manuscript underscoring as an instruction for italics, not a reason to underline a finished digital document.
- Under the selected literal style, omit an initial A or The from a literary title after a preceding possessive. Keep an exact quoted title, bibliographic entry, or official source identifier intact.

## Layout and final form

- Leave a blank line or equivalent spacing after a heading. The first-line rule for a succeeding ruled-paper page applies only when editing that physical medium.
- Use figures or appropriate Roman numerals for dates and serial designations. Do not arbitrarily spell out a date or numbered unit.
- Inspect word division only when typesetting makes it relevant. Divide according to formation, syllables, and double consonants, respecting simple-word boundaries. Avoid an isolated single letter or very short fragment at a line end. Let the renderer hyphenate; never insert manual line-break hyphens into Markdown prose. Use a reliable division dictionary for unfamiliar words, because no mechanical rule settles every case.
