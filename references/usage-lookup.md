# Usage and spelling lookup

Consult this lookup when a word or construction needs a decision. Apply the stated sense and register, not a blind substitution. These are the selected usage preferences, including literal historical restrictions. Preserve names, quotations, exact titles, adopted terminology, and explicitly protected wording. Flag a protected conflict instead of silently changing it. The complete house bans take priority over a permissive usage entry.

## Word and construction decisions

| Wording | Editorial decision |
|---|---|
| All right | Write two words. Reserve it for detached familiar assent or permission; choose a precise expression for other uses. |
| As good or better than | Make both comparisons grammatical, such as “as accurate as the first account, or more accurate.” |
| As to whether | Use whether unless the surrounding grammar needs a different construction. |
| Bid | In the command sense, use an infinitive without to and the past bade. Do not apply this restriction to a bidding-price sense or an exact quotation. |
| Case | Cut empty in-many-cases or it-is-the-case-that framing. Retain an actual instance, legal case, grammatical case, or technical meaning. |
| Certainly | Remove empty intensification even once. Retain a degree of assurance only when evidence supports it and the reader needs it under Q1; do not insert uncertainty into a verified fact. |
| Character | Cut redundant of-a-character phrasing. Retain an actual quality, person, or technical object. |
| Claim | Apply the complete house ban to the lexical family, including an otherwise legitimate entitlement sense. Protect exact names, titles, quotations, and explicitly retained wording. |
| Compare | Use compare to for resemblance across different orders of things and compare with for a comparison mainly of differences within the same order. Preserve the actual analytical operation. |
| Clever | Restrict it to ingenuity in small matters. Name the achievement for broader praise. |
| Consider | Omit as when consider means believe to be. Retain as when it introduces the role or capacity under discussion. |
| Dependable | Mandatory house ban, even once. Recast with reliable, trustworthy, or other accurate wording; preserve protected names, titles, quotations, and author decisions, flagging the conflict. |
| Due to | Use as a predicate or modifier tied to a noun. Replace an adverbial causal use with because of or owing to without changing the causal assertion. |
| Effect | Separate noun result, verb bring about, and affect meaning influence. Replace vague artistic effects with a concrete description supported by the text. |
| Etc. | Do not use for persons, omitted important particulars, or after such as or for example. Retain only when the continuation is clear and immaterial. Preserve a quoted abbreviation. |
| Fact | Reserve for directly verifiable matters. Describe judgments as judgments, remove the fact that padding, and verify factual additions. |
| Factor | Name the actual supported cause or contribution instead of a vague factor formulation. Retain precise mathematical and analytical meanings. |
| Feature | State the attribute or action instead of vague praise. Avoid the verb's advertising sense of promoting a special attraction. |
| Fix | In authored prose, restrict it to fastening or making firm. Use arrange, prepare, mend, or another exact verb for colloquial senses. This preference does not prohibit editorial instructions from referring to a proposed fix. |
| He is a man who | Remove an empty person-or-thing classification and attach the useful predicate to the subject. Preserve a classification that contributes meaning. |
| However | When it means nevertheless, place it within the clause. Keep an initial however when it means in whatever way or to whatever extent. Do not replace it with a new habitual connective. |
| Kind of | Keep the literal category sense. Do not use it as a degree qualifier before adjectives or verbs. Before a noun, an approximate sense meaning something like is acceptable only in familiar style. Apply the same distinctions to sort of. |
| Less | Use fewer for countable number and less for quantity or degree. Retain an amount-like exception for a round total, such as a number below a hundred. |
| Line, along these lines | State the procedure, subject, or argument instead of an overworked metaphor. Preserve a literal line or precise technical term. |
| Literal, literally | Do not intensify a figurative exaggeration with literal. Retain an actual statement about literal meaning. |
| Lose out | Cut out or up where it adds no meaning, checking try out, win out, sign up, and register up in context. Preserve a phrasal verb with a different meaning from the simple verb, such as trying a product experimentally. |
| Most | Do not substitute it for almost. Retain a superlative or majority sense. |
| Nature | Cut empty of-a-nature phrasing. Name the scenery, setting, organism, or other actual subject hidden by vague nature language. |
| Nearby | Use near or near at hand adverbially and neighboring adjectivally. |
| Oftentimes | Use often. |
| One hundred and one | Keep and in this and analogous compound number expressions. Preserve numeric identifiers and direct quotations. |
| One of the most | Do not open a paragraph or essay with this worn formula. State the particular development or subject. |
| People | Use people for the political sense and the public for an audience or market under the selected distinction. Use persons with a stated number. Preserve other precise ordinary or group senses outside that restriction. |
| Phase | Use for a stage of change or development. Name a static aspect, topic, or part more precisely. |
| Possess | Use have or own when possess adds no meaning. Retain precise legal or technical usage. |
| Respective, respectively | Cut needless distributive wording or repeat the actual pairing. Retain respectively in a technical proof where it supplies an unambiguous mapping that would otherwise be cumbersome. |
| So | Avoid it as a degree intensifier in authored prose. Check connective so for clause-boundary errors and habitual use without deleting a meaningful consequence. |
| Sort of | Keep the literal category sense. Do not use it as a degree qualifier before adjectives or verbs. Allow a noun approximation meaning something like only in familiar style, as with kind of. |
| State | Use say or an exact reporting verb for a mere speech attribution. Retain state for expressing something fully or clearly. |
| Student body | Use students or a student when the collective expression adds nothing. Preserve an institutional title or meaningful collective distinction. |
| System | Cut when the named arrangement already conveys what the sentence needs. Retain an actual system and adopted technical terminology. |
| Thanking you in advance | Avoid presuming that a request will be granted. Use an appropriate thanks, then acknowledge a favor after receipt. Editing a message does not authorize sending it. |
| They | For a singular antecedent in new prose, use a plural antecedent, repeat a role noun, or recast the sentence. Do not assign an unknown person's gender, change stated personal pronouns, or alter a quotation. |
| Very | Use sparingly. Choose a precise word or supported degree when emphasis matters. |
| Viewpoint | Use point of view for perspective and view or opinion for a judgment. |
| While | Apply the complete house ban, including temporal and concessive uses. Protect exact quotations, names, titles, and explicitly retained wording. |
| Whom | Determine the pronoun's role inside its own clause. Use who as the subject of a following verb even when an inserted he said or similar phrase intervenes. Use whom for an actual object. |
| Worthwhile | Apply to an action, not as an adjective before a noun. Describe the value or use worth plus an action where appropriate. |
| Would | Use should for the selected first-person conditional convention and for indirect shall after a past reporting verb. Use simple past for ordinary habitual action when the modal adds nothing. Preserve genuine conditional differences, quotations, and stated author exceptions. |

### Usage boundary examples

- **Degree versus category:** “The task is kind of difficult.” → “The task is somewhat difficult” only when the supported degree is necessary. Otherwise use “The task is difficult.” Do not automatically replace one unnecessary qualifier with another; apply [Q1](model-style-review.md#q1-keep-only-necessary-qualifications). “A seminar is a kind of class” uses the literal category sense and should stay. In familiar writing, “a kind of workshop” can be an acceptable noun approximation; it does not license **kind of difficult**. Apply the same distinction to **sort of**.
- **Causal versus noun-related due to:** Under the selected literal usage, change “We postponed the trip due to fog” to “We postponed the trip because of fog.” Keep “The postponement was due to fog.” Do not replace every occurrence without checking its grammatical role.
- **Subject versus object pronoun:** “The guest whom Mina said would arrive” → “The guest who Mina said would arrive.” The pronoun is the subject of **would arrive**. Keep “the guest whom Mina invited,” where the pronoun is the object of **invited**.

## Spelling review

Check correct spelling in context. A listed correct form is not an error or a banned word. Respect the requested English variety and verify unfamiliar terminology. Do not turn every British -ise ending into -ize. Philip is an example of a personal name, not authority to change another person's spelling. Proofread unlisted words too.

Correct-form checklist, alphabetized for lookup:

Accidentally, advice, affect, beginning, believe, benefit, challenge, criticize, deceive, definite, describe, despise, develop, disappoint, duel, ecstasy, effect, existence, fiery, formerly, humorous, hypocrisy, immediately, incidentally, latter, led, lose, marriage, mischief, murmur, necessary, occurred, parallel, Philip, playwright, preceding, prejudice, principal, privilege, pursue, repetition, rhyme, rhythm, ridiculous, sacrilegious, seize, separate, shepherd, siege, simile, similar, too, tragedy, tries, undoubtedly, until.

## Confused forms

| Forms | Check the intended meaning |
|---|---|
| advice / advise | Advice is the noun; advise is the corresponding verb. |
| affect / effect | Affect commonly means influence. Effect is a result as a noun or bring about as a verb. |
| formerly / formally | Formerly means previously; formally concerns form or convention. |
| latter / later | Latter names the second of two. Do not use it in place of later. |
| led / lead | Led is the past tense in the guiding sense. Separate the metal and the present-tense verb. |
| lose / loose | Lose means fail to keep or win; loose means unfastened or not tight. |
| principal / principle | Principal can mean chief, a school head, or a sum of money. Principle means a rule or belief. |
| preceding / proceeding | Preceding means coming before; proceeding can mean continuing or a process. |
| too / to / two | Too means also or excessively. Check the preposition or infinitive marker to and the number two by function. |
| duel / dual | Duel names a contest; dual means involving two. |
| simile | Use for an explicit comparison and preserve the subject's correct terminology. |
