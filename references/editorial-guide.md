# Editorial review guide

Use this guide to review existing text and to self-edit new writing. Start with the reader's task and the text's actual genre. Check meaning, organization, sentence construction, clarity, repetition, and correctness. Preserve the writer's voice and deliberate choices. Rule IDs keep checker reports traceable; source history lives separately in [provenance](provenance.md).

Existing author wording receives proposed edits before replacement. Quotations, cited titles, proper names, adopted terminology, and explicitly protected wording are exempt from automatic normalization. Flag conflicts instead of damaging meaning.

## Review purpose and organization

Confirm what the reader needs to understand or do. Each paragraph develops one coherent point; a paragraph break marks a useful change in that development. A small subject may need one paragraph and a complex subject several. In sustained prose, check isolated one-sentence paragraphs for a real transition, emphasis, or change of speaker. Do not manufacture uniform paragraph lengths or impose an essay outline on an email.

In dialogue, begin a new paragraph at each change of speaker, even for a one-word reply. Preserve any explicitly requested quotation or transcript format.

Open explanatory paragraphs with their actual point, then develop it through supported facts, explanation, definition, examples, or consequences. Narrative passages can start with an actor and action. End where the substantive point or evidence finishes; add no redundant closing verdict. Give important information a useful position, often at a sentence's end, without forced inversion or opaque delayed subjects.

For example, when the board's decision is the point, “The board approved the repairs after a long debate” can become “After a long debate, the board approved the repairs.” Move only the timing phrase. If the debate is the intended emphasis, keep the original order. Emphasis depends on the surrounding paragraph; the editor should not move phrases mechanically.

### FLOW Develop a connected paragraph

Review the paragraph as a whole before proposing a repair and again after local fixes. A passage must develop a thought the reader can follow. Correct grammar and the absence of banned phrases do not suffice when the result reads like disconnected notes.

- **Follow the development.** Identify the paragraph's point or action, then check what each sentence adds: explanation, evidence, an example, a necessary limit, a consequence, or the next event. Give the reader enough context to follow the progression. These are possible functions, not a mandatory sequence or a template for every paragraph.
- **Connect given and new information.** Carry a clear subject, referent, or needed term from one sentence into the next before introducing another topic. Retain useful repetition, articles, pronouns, and clause markers that help the reader follow the relation. Explain a supported connection where the reader would otherwise have to reconstruct it.
- **Shape sentences to the thought.** Combine related short statements, subordinate a supporting detail, or move a phrase when that makes the relation clearer. Keep a short sentence for a real emphasis or action. Avoid both a string of clipped declarations and an overloaded chain of clauses. Match the author's tone and genre instead of making all prose terse.
- **Use transitions for their meaning.** A connector, shared subject, relative clause, or reordered detail can express the relation. Use only relationships supported by the source. Joining facts does not authorize inventing a cause, chronology, contrast, intention, or outcome. Never add a stock transition merely to disguise a gap in the argument.
- **Read across boundaries.** Check how the paragraph follows the preceding one and where it leaves the reader. Let the ending finish the actual development; do not append a slogan, redundant recap, or automatic caveat. Keep a necessary limit where it qualifies the relevant assertion, under Q1.

Accept the passage only when the reader can follow why each sentence comes next and how the paragraph advances its purpose. Sentence-length statistics cannot make that judgment. If the source lacks the substance needed for a connection, flag the gap instead of manufacturing a bridge. Procedural steps and concise notices can appropriately use short, parallel sentences; no universal sentence-length target or compulsory narrative arc applies.

A surgical repair may span two or more adjacent sentences when their relationship is the problem. Propose the smallest connected span that fixes that relationship, leaving effective surrounding material alone. For existing text, keep the approval boundary: a newly needed join or reorder requires a new or revised proposal; it cannot silently expand an already approved replacement.

**Worked example.** The task is an evaluation update covering the release plan, verified function, and evidence about review time. Assume a June release is a plan, the tool's comparison capability is verified, reviewers use it when checking revisions, and the pilot leaves its effect on review time unresolved.

Before: “We plan to release the tool in June. Reviewers can use it to compare documents side by side. The pilot has not confirmed time savings.”

After: “We plan a June release for the tool, which lets reviewers compare documents side by side as they check revisions. The pilot results still leave us uncertain about whether the tool saves them time.”

The relative clause links the release to the tool's function, and the final sentence addresses the unresolved benefit required by this brief. A simple launch announcement with no need to assess time savings would not automatically need that caveat; apply Q1 to the actual purpose. The plan stays a plan, the capability stays verified, and the result stays uncertain. This is a contextual example of connected development, not a fixed paragraph formula.

## Review sentence construction

Use the actual actor and a direct verb. Remove empty predicates, nominalizations, and there-is openings when they obscure the action. An active-voice requirement never permits inventing an actor. Keep meaningful negatives and qualifications; positive phrasing must preserve the original logical scope.

Cut words that add no information, not words the reader needs to understand the relation. Concision does not require uniformly short sentences. Combine fragmented explanations when that helps and split overloaded sentences only where the thought needs it. Vary recurring two-clause sentences and connectors according to their actual relations; preserve an effective isolated instance.

### Surgical concision examples

- **Remove an empty phrase:** “Because of the fact that the road flooded, we changed routes.” → “Because the road flooded, we changed routes.” Keep the stated cause; do not change the strength of the causal assertion.
- **Let the verb carry the action:** “Nora conducted a review of the file.” → “Nora reviewed the file.” Keep the actor and object. Preserve a formal procedure name if the document uses **review** in a specialized sense.
- **Reduce a redundant relative wrapper:** “Nora, who is the treasurer, signed the form.” → “Nora, the treasurer, signed the form.” Remove **who is** only when it adds no necessary tense, contrast, or qualification. Apply the same judgment to **which was** and similar wrappers.
- **Combine a fragmented thought:** “The pilot lasted two weeks. During those two weeks, the team tested three routes.” → “During the two-week pilot, the team tested three routes.” Keep every substantive fact. Use this only when combining the sentences helps the thought; do not remove repetition that guides the reader.

### Vary recurring sentence structures

When several adjacent sentences all use two coordinated clauses, change only enough of them to remove the mechanical rhythm. Read the paragraph under FLOW before deciding whether joining, subordination, reordering, or splitting serves its meaning; splitting is not the default repair. For example, in a paragraph that repeatedly follows **X happened, and Y happened**, “The meeting ended, and the staff left” can become “The meeting ended. The staff left.” Other sentences may retain their original shape. Do not substitute **because**, **although**, or another more specific relation unless the source supports it. Keep an isolated clear two-clause sentence and useful grammatical parallelism.

Check clause boundaries, modifier attachment, related-word proximity, parallel grammar, tense, and attribution using [grammar and form](grammar-and-form.md). Consult [usage and spelling](usage-lookup.md) for lexical decisions.

## Check reader processing and unnecessary compression

Check P1–P6 during an audit, when proposing or applying surgical edits, and during the writer's final self-review. Diagnose a real reading obstacle in context; a construction is not defective merely because it matches a category. Use the smallest repair that removes the obstacle and keep surrounding wording. Preserve facts, actor roles, chronology, uncertainty, emphasis, protected text, and technical meaning. Re-read the repaired sentence in its paragraph. In propose mode, give the exact OLD/NEW span and the relevant P-code; apply only approved edits to adopted text.

### P1 Helpful clause and relation markers

Retain or restore small grammatical words when they make the relationship easier to follow, even when omission is grammatical. Check optional **that**, relation-marking prepositions such as **about**, **of**, **to**, and **for**, articles, auxiliaries, and conjunctions. A word can help the reader parse a relationship without adding a new fact. Do not strip that help merely to shorten a sentence or treat it as a gratuitous qualification under Q1.

- **Clause boundary:** “The memo the manager sent explains the delay.” → “The memo that the manager sent explains the delay.” Insert **that** when it helps the reader follow the clause; retain an already clear unmarked clause where appropriate.
- **Relation and academic register:** “The pilot results still leave us unsure whether the tool saves them time.” → “The pilot results still leave us uncertain about whether the tool saves them time.” **About** makes the relation explicit. The user generally prefers **uncertain** over **unsure** in academic prose; this is a register preference, not a universal ban on **unsure**. Preserve the same degree of uncertainty.

Apply the same judgment to other function words. Preserve an article's definiteness, an auxiliary's tense or modality, and a connective's actual logical relation. Restore a missing preposition only when the intended relation is known; use P3 for ambiguous noun strings. Do not add every optional word, invent a causal or temporal connection, or make prose ceremonious. Prefer the fuller phrasing when it helps this reader follow the thought.

### P2 Embedded clauses and distant related words

Watch for nested relative clauses, competing actors, or long interruptions between a subject and its main verb. Bring related words together, move an interruption, or split only the overloaded clause. Preserve event order, logical scope, and emphasis. Sentence length alone does not determine difficulty.

Example: “The manager, after checking the forms and consulting the team, approved the request.” → “After checking the forms and consulting the team, the manager approved the request.” Make this change when the interruption obstructs reading; do not normalize every sentence into the same order.

### P3 Opaque noun strings

Watch for successive noun modifiers that hide the relation among things or permit competing interpretations. Spell out that relation with a preposition or a short clause. Three or more noun modifiers are a cue to inspect, not an automatic violation. Preserve familiar compounds, official names, and adopted technical terms. If the relation is uncertain, ask instead of inventing it.

Example: “library volunteer training schedule” → “schedule for training library volunteers.” Revise only the noun phrase, and only if this expresses the intended relation. Use this as the concrete form of the existing compressed-referent check, not a second reason to edit the same span twice.

### P4 Improvised noun-to-verb conversions

Watch for ad hoc verbs that make the reader infer an unfamiliar action from a noun. Replace the invented use with a familiar verb phrase naming the same action. Keep conventional verbs such as **email** and precise technical uses when the audience understands them.

Example: “Calendar the review.” → “Add the review to the calendar.” Preserve the requested action and its object; do not replace every verb that historically came from a noun.

### P5 Strained personification and subject–verb fit

Watch for an object, document, process, or abstraction taking a verb that implies intentions or actions the subject cannot plausibly have. State the supported relation or name the real actor when known. Keep ordinary technical descriptions such as “the function returns a value” and useful, clear metonymy. Do not invent an actor or causal explanation.

Example: “The policy wants staff to report faults.” → “The policy requires staff to report faults,” only if the policy actually imposes a requirement. If it recommends reporting, preserve that weaker force. Uncertain meaning calls for a question, not an automatic replacement.

### P6 Unfamiliar compressed labels

Watch for novel compounds, odd word combinations, and shorthand that make the reader decode a private label. Use familiar wording and make the actual relation explicit, even when this needs a few more words. Keep necessary technical vocabulary and named concepts. Concision means removing needless wording, not every predictable word.

Example: “The team hit a decision-density ceiling.” → “The team had too many decisions to make at once.” Confirm that this is the intended meaning before using the repair. Prefer familiar wording even when it repeats a useful phrase. Do not force a novel synonym merely to satisfy phrase variation.

These are contextual editorial applications of research on linguistic processing, not an authorship detector, numerical readability score, or validated guarantee of improved comprehension. The read-only checker lists them as manual review requirements; it does not syntactically detect them. Source details and evidence limits are in [provenance](provenance.md#reader-processing-checks).

## Apply word and punctuation restrictions

Enforce the [mandatory Claude and Astra phrase bans](model-style-review.md#mandatory-claude-and-astra-phrase-bans) in addition to the restrictions below. They apply even once. Remove unnecessary qualifications; retain or recast one only when its removal would mislead, overstate the evidence, or change a relevant condition. Preserve meaning and genuine uncertainty through local recasts. Protected wording and edit approvals keep their existing treatment.

Case-insensitive bans include inflected and derived forms of **claim, reveal, appear, examine, emerge, remain, while, whilst, meanwhile, awhile, rather, durable, distinguish, identify, distinct, concern, asks, beyond, quiet, establish**. Apply the source's inflection rule to the verb family `ask/asks/asked/asking`; a question in an editing conversation is outside the prose being edited. Review unfamiliar derivatives in context. For `quiet` used to describe toleration, prefer `informal` when accurate.

Ban **the central argument of** and **rather than**. State the substance directly. Avoid **supply** and **provide** as generic descriptions of what a case, source, or literature does. Name the action. Also review **offer, assess, record** when they fill the same empty role. These general verb checks are contextual; lexical bans and the named phrase bans still override a precise or concrete use. In particular, **may provide** and **can provide** always require recasts in unprotected prose. A physical supply or a record as a noun can remain outside those bans.

Avoid habitual **merely**, **mere**, and near variants; one isolated, useful occurrence is acceptable. Replace compressed relations such as “X concerns who controls...” with a sentence that explains the relation. Avoid repeating one substitute everywhere.

Use no em dashes. Use no colons joining clauses in running prose or table cells. Headings, references, cited titles, metadata, times, URLs, code, and syntax-required notation are outside this prose punctuation rule. Rebuild clauses with an accurate connective or a sentence boundary.

Remove empty **not X but Y**, **it is not X, it is Y**, and trailing **not Y** structures; state Y. If the contrast carries real meaning, preserve that meaning through positive restructuring, without pretending it never existed.

Do not write that a source **leaves open**, **left open**, **leaves unexplained**, or **leaves unanswered** a question, including rearranged forms. State actively what the source does not explain or which evidence the answer needs. Never turn this into an unsupported novelty assertion.

Active voice is an absolute house requirement for new prose and proposed NEW spans. Name the actual actor. Never invent an actor to remove a passive. When the actor is unknown, recast accurately or flag the limitation. Quotations, titles, and explicitly protected text retain their existing exemptions. Avoid section-number cross-references in running prose; briefly restate the substance or name the section. Name the document by its actual type and purpose. Use conventional terminology, not invented shorthand labels.

Mechanical word matching needs contextual review. The source both mentions derived forms and gives **distinction** as a false positive for **distinct**. Treat `distinction` as a contextual lexical-family question rather than a definite automatic violation. Do not stem blindly or change unrelated prefix matches. The checker lists known families; manual review must catch unlisted derivatives.

## Check coherence and continuity

Start each prose paragraph with a topic sentence stating its point, not a question. Keep one coherent idea per paragraph. Introduce unfamiliar people, organizations, events, and terms at first use for the actual audience. For an exchange or bargain, name who gives what and who receives what. Split overloaded sentences where their logic becomes hard to follow. Do not turn every long sentence into two medium ones.

Apply [Q1, qualification necessity](model-style-review.md#q1-keep-only-necessary-qualifications): delete gratuitous caveats, even a single occurrence. Retain a limit only when omission would materially mislead, overstate the evidence, or change a relevant condition. State a necessary limit once within its explanation. Keep episodes and dates in intelligible order and end on evidence, substantive development, or consequence. Remove empty images and redundant verdicts. Give each substantive point one home; search nearby passages before adding another occurrence.

## Remove generic rhetorical templates

| ID | Diagnose | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| A3.1 Repeated limitation formula | Repeated “these accounts explain X; they do not explain Y” endings. | State the relevant difference, unmet need, actual question, or next substantive point. Verify any research-gap assertion when the task is scholarly. |
| A3.2 Numbered setup | Repeated announcements of two implications or three features. | Begin with the substance; retain numbering only when readers need an actual enumerated structure. |
| A3.3 Ranking sources | Formulaic strongest, closest, or sharpest source rankings. | Explain the relevant contribution or difference instead of ranking sources for effect. |
| A3.4 Missing-knowledge opener | Artificial “what is not known” or “what is missing” leads. | Name who needs which information or what the evidence does not tell us. |
| A3.5 Throat clearing | Roadmaps and writing-process commentary without substantive content. | Delete or replace with the actual point. |
| A3.6 Templated section opening | Each section follows the same explains/fails/this-work-takes sequence. | Let the argument, event, evidence, or relation determine each opening. |

For these contextual template checks, a single useful construction is not automatically a defect. This allowance never overrides a lexical or named phrase ban, or Q1's requirement to delete an unnecessary qualification even once.

## Make the meaning concrete

Replace vague abstractions with the actual thing, action, decision, or supporting fact. Name actors, dates, indicators, and examples only when the source supports them. Explain why a real problem matters. State the significance plainly and explain technical concepts without substituting jargon for an argument. If a reader could reasonably respond “meaning what?”, finish the explanation.

## Verify content and attribution when relevant

When a passage discusses a report, study, article, interview, or other source, explain the relevant content accurately and make its relation to the point clear. Include only what the reader needs. Keep the source's own statement separate from the writer's interpretation. For scholarly analysis, its question, argument, evidence, mechanism, and finding may be necessary; this is a conditional task, not the default structure for every document. Avoid vague glosses that conceal the actual content.

Verify factual additions using supplied evidence or retrieved sources. Cite empirical assertions, dates, and quotations where the genre requires citations. Keep text and reference list consistent using the document's citation system. Never add an unsupported causal bridge or replace a precise source description with a flattering generalization. Do not manufacture facts to make prose concrete.

Use accepted terms and preserve adopted terminology. Explain specialized roles or concepts when the reader needs them. Separate plans, predictions, interpretations, and observed results. Do not present a plan as a completed result or obscure contrary evidence. Use the requested genre's prose, lists, tables, and length. Research-design checks apply only when the actual task requires them.

## Review formulaic phrasing in context

| ID | Diagnose | Surgical move |
|---|---|---|
| A10.1 Defensive inability | “X alone does not show Y,” “cannot stand in for,” “does not by itself,” “cannot be answered from,” “cannot be determined from.” | Delete the caveat if it fails Q1. Only when its limit is necessary, state the needed evidence or the actual finding without changing evidential strength; never invent a new research gap or reassurance. |
| A10.2 Stamp ending | Flat concluding verdicts, especially repeated summaries. | Join necessary content to its explanation, or end on the actual evidence. |
| A10.3 All-purpose verb | Supply, provide, establish, offer, assess, record used without a concrete action. | Enforce lexical and named phrase bans even for precise uses. For the other contextual verbs, replace filler with the actual action without repeating one substitute. |
| A10.4 Repeated opener | Repeated document, project, or method labels or bare “This” pointing at a whole thought. | Name the referent or vary the sentence's actual subject. |
| A10.5 Trailing contrast | A final “not Y” answers an objection nobody made. | Keep the positive point and remove the empty contrast. |
| A10.6 Stacked conditional | Runs of two or three “If” sentences read like a decision tree. | Vary sentence structure without changing the logical conditions. |
| A10.7 Glue opener | Recurring “Either,” “not just,” “however,” “therefore,” “moreover,” or “thus.” | Express the relation concretely; retain a connective when it actually guides the reader. |
| A10.8 Numbered/caveat setup | Formulaic “two examples show” or “neither...since” moves. | Enter the substance or explanation directly. |
| A10.9 Repeated example template | Different examples, cases, or events repeat the same sentence and paragraph skeleton. | Explain each in a structure suited to its substance. Keep correct grammatical parallelism within equivalent items. |
| A10.10 Polished one-liner | Aphorism substitutes for analysis. | State the specific relation or remove the line if it adds nothing. |
| A10.11 Jargon for substance | Specialist or procedural language conceals what happened, what works, or why it matters. | Name the actual action and relation in plain language; preserve necessary technical precision. |
| A10.12 Vague relation | “Pointed in different directions,” “the two,” or unspecified differences. | Name both things and what each did or implied. |
| A10.13 Stock phrase | “Sheds light on,” “speaks to,” “in its own right,” or a question a source “leaves open.” | State the actual contribution or delete empty wording. |

## Resolve actors, objects, and referents

Every action of blocking, removing, granting, withdrawing, accepting, or refusing needs its actor and object. Every `this`, `it`, `the two`, `either`, and `such` needs an unambiguous referent in the same or preceding sentence. Unpack noun stacks and one-word compressions when they hide the action, occasion, or relationship. Define a technical term plainly at first mention. A needed explanation may take a long sentence. Do not shorten it into an opaque abstraction.

## Remove redundancy and preserve rhythm

Preserve necessary substance when deleting a sentence. For redundancy, explain what it repeats and where that point survives. For a gratuitous caveat, cite its failure of Q1; an unnecessary qualification need not survive elsewhere merely because it has literal meaning. Never delete evidence, a relevant condition, or genuine uncertainty under this allowance. Allow useful repetition whenever it makes the text easier to follow, including repeated nouns, conventional phrases, necessary terminology, and clear parallel instructions. Do not replace clear wording with synonyms solely to avoid recurring three-word phrases across edits. Flag repeated empty rhetorical templates and mechanical repair formulas when they add no content or obstruct the point. Vary the wording or structure only when the change improves clarity or natural flow; no blanket phrase-uniqueness rule applies.

Measure sentence-length spread before and after substantial rounds. Preserve broad natural variation where the author has it, including short and long sentences. The source's historical averages and under-eight/over-forty examples describe an author's rhythm; they are not quotas for every email or paragraph. Do not insert padding, fragments, mistakes, or needless length to manufacture variation. Devices such as “But,” “So,” “And,” “Still,” first person, merging, and splitting should follow the thought and should not become replacement templates.

## Review habits across models

During a full review, follow [the model-independent style review](model-style-review.md) for recurring vocabulary and framing (M1), formatting and organization (M2), and syntax and rhythm (M3). Use the actual text and suitable reference samples; a model name alone cannot diagnose a defect. Check for new habits introduced by repairs. The named Claude/Astra phrase bans are mandatory; other model-style checks remain contextual. They supplement all existing bans and patterns.

## Detector reports

Use a supplied report only within the requested task. Match its flagged passages to the current draft using exact or carefully verified near-exact correspondence. Target only currently unchanged flagged text; report uncertain matches, counts, and sections already being revised. Preserve disclosures of AI assistance. A score or label is not evidence about who wrote a passage.
